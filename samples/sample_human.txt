I still remember the first time I visited my grandfather's farm out in Nebraska. It was hot—ridiculously hot—and the smell of dry earth and old tractor grease hit you the second you opened the screen door. He wasn't running some massive commercial outfit; it was just forty acres of corn and a stubborn garden that my grandmother refused to give up on. But even then, back in the late nineties, you could already tell things were changing fast around the county. The neighboring farms were getting swallowed up by big agribusiness combines, tearing down the hedgerows and spraying everything in sight with chemical cocktails that left the ditches dead quiet. Where were the meadowlarks? Why didn't we hear frogs down by the creek anymore? It's honestly infuriating when corporate lobbyists pretend that modern industrial farming is the only way to feed the world. That's just nonsense. When you look at how much food actually gets wasted before it ever hits a grocery store shelf, you realize the whole system is built on artificial surplus and cheap subsidies. We're trading away topsoil that took ten thousand years to form just so someone can squeeze out an extra five bushels an acre this quarter! My grandfather used to say that if you treat the dirt like dirt, that's exactly what you'll leave behind for your kids. He was right. We don't need more chemical inputs; we need common sense and farmers who actually care about the land they're standing on.
