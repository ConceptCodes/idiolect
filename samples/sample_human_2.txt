Every summer when school let out, my cousins and I would pile into the back of my uncle's beat-up pickup truck. We didn't have cell phones or video games back then; we just had fifty acres of creek beds and an old rope swing hanging from a dying willow tree. My uncle always joked that if you weren't covered in mud and mosquito bites by noon, you weren't doing summer right. He wasn't one of those fancy commercial growers with GPS-guided tractors and corporate contracts. He farmed the old-fashioned way, rotating his soybeans and alfalfa and letting the back pasture sit fallow whenever the soil started feeling tired. Why do people nowadays think everything has to be optimized within an inch of its life? It's completely exhausting. When you talk to the folks running the giant seed syndicates down at the state capital, they talk about agriculture like it's a software company. They don't see the soil as living; they see it as a factory floor. But you can't trick nature forever, can you? Sooner or later, the bill comes due. We're draining the Ogallala aquifer faster than it can ever recharge, and for what? Just to grow ethanol corn that nobody eats! If we don't start listening to the older generation of stewards who treated the ground with respect, we won't have any family farms left to pass along.
